## 2. No-natural-section theorem

If a nontrivial group \(G\) acts freely and vertically on a fibre while acting
trivially on \(B\), no \(G\)-equivariant section \(s:B\to E\) exists.

Indeed, equivariance would imply

\[

\]

## 3. Invariant-minimizer theorem

Let \(J:E\to\mathbb R\) be \(G\)-invariant. If \(x\) minimizes \(J\), then

\[
J(gx)=J(x)

\]

for all \(g\in G\). Hence the full orbit \(Gx\) consists of minimizers.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{a unique minimizer of an invariant action must be a fixed point of }G.}

\]

On a free realization torsor no such point exists. Strict convexity cannot
override this theorem: either the domain is not invariant, the action is not
invariant, the orbit is not free, or an explicit symmetry-breaking term has
been added.

This applies directly to minimum-tail, spectral-action, least-action and
free-energy proposals. A variational principle can select a realization, but
the part of the functional that distinguishes fibre points is precisely the
missing section resource.

## 4. Invariant-measure theorem

If \(J\) is invariant, the Gibbs density \(e^{-\beta J}\) is invariant. On a
finite compact orbit it assigns equal probability to symmetry-related branches.
Its zero-temperature limit concentrates on the entire minimizer orbit, not one
member.

For noncompact translation and scale torsors there is no finite invariant Haar
probability. Normalization requires a prior, cutoff or confining potential,
which again supplies extra realization structure.

Thus probability replaces a deterministic section by a symmetric mixture or a
prior-dependent random section; it does not eliminate the source problem.
